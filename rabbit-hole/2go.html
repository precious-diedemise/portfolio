What Happened to 2go?

“Preciousred.”

That was my 2go username.

Weird name, right? Well, all the good names were taken. Even Preciouswhite. Apparently, I wasn't the only Precious trying to make a name for herself on the internet.

That was how popular 2go was.

For millions of young people across Africa and beyond, including countries like India and Indonesia, 2go wasn't just another messaging app. It was the place to be. The chat rooms, the usernames, the friendships with people you'd probably never meet, and the excitement of seeing someone come online.

If you were around between 2011 and 2013, chances are you either had a 2go account or knew someone who wouldn't stop talking about theirs.

And then came BlackBerry Messenger (BBM), WhatsApp, smartphones, and a completely different era of messaging.

But something has always bothered me about 2go's story.

Why didn't it evolve?

Or perhaps the better question is: Did 2go fail to evolve, or was it simply built for a world that was disappearing?

A Little Background Before We Investigate

So, a little background about the app before we try to figure out what happened.

2go was founded in 2007 by two computer science students, Alan Wolff and Ashley Peter, who studied at the University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg, South Africa.

Interestingly, it wasn't originally meant to be a messaging app. The idea was much simpler: help students keep track of their lecture schedules. But somewhere along the way, the founders realized that students weren't just interested in knowing where their next lecture was. They wanted to communicate with one another.

And that was how 2go gradually became a messaging platform.

What makes its story particularly interesting is that it didn't need a massive advertising budget to become popular. People simply told other people about it.

By 2012, 2go had grown to over 20 million registered users, with more than 10 million active users across its major African markets.

Think about that for a moment.

Two university students had built a platform that millions of people were using to communicate, largely through word of mouth.

But why did it work so well?

For one, 2go understood the market it was serving. At a time when smartphones were still relatively expensive and internet access wasn't exactly cheap, it allowed people to communicate using basic mobile phones while consuming very little data.

And 2go was more than just chatting.

There were chat rooms, game rooms, leaderboards, and those famous star levels that some people took very seriously.

Then there were GoCredits, the virtual currency users could purchase to access certain features and activities. There were also status updates, photos, and communities built around shared interests.

Looking back, 2go had built an entire social experience. There was community, competition, entertainment, and even a virtual economy.

So, What Actually Happened to 2go?

One thing I find particularly interesting about 2go's decline is how quietly it happened.

There wasn't a dramatic announcement that everyone was leaving. No major scandal that I remember. We simply stopped logging in.

If I remember correctly, BlackBerry Messenger was one of the first platforms that began stealing our attention. Suddenly, we weren't just exchanging 2go usernames anymore. People were exchanging BBM PINs.

And eventually, WhatsApp became the place to be.

It wasn't necessarily that we had grown to dislike 2go. Our friends were simply somewhere else.

But blaming BBM or WhatsApp alone feels a little too convenient.

The founders weren't oblivious to the changing mobile market. By June 2013, 2go had launched an Android app. So, contrary to what I initially thought, 2go did evolve.

But perhaps evolving and evolving fast enough are two different things.

Could 2go have invested in smartphones earlier? Could its chat rooms, games, leaderboards, and virtual currency have become the foundation for something bigger? Or would competing against emerging platforms have been difficult regardless?

There is also the question of what people actually wanted. 2go encouraged users to meet new people, explore communities, and create online identities. WhatsApp, on the other hand, made it incredibly easy to communicate with people already in your phone contacts.

Was the future of messaging becoming less about meeting strangers and more about staying connected to people we already knew?

Same Era, Different Futures

There's another platform I remember using around the same time: Facebook.

In fact, I think I opened my Facebook and 2go accounts around the same period. Both were popular, and we somehow managed to enjoy them on those little phones with tiny screens and physical keypads.

Interestingly, in 2012, reports suggested that 2go had more users in Nigeria than Facebook.

So, here's another question: If both platforms were popular around the same time, why did Facebook go on to become a global social media giant while 2go gradually faded from mainstream attention?

Perhaps comparing the two isn't entirely fair. But it raises another useful question: Does a company's future depend more on how well it serves its existing users or how well it anticipates what those users will eventually want?

I Downloaded 2go Again. In 2026.

And, well, I wasn't expecting what I found.

I couldn't remember anything about my old account. Preciousred was officially beyond my reach.

So I tried creating a new account. Registration failed. Then I switched my country selection to Nigeria. And it worked.

Suddenly, I was back on 2go, and surprisingly, it felt a lot like the 2go I remembered.

There were still chat rooms, with categories like languages, football, and entertainment, with smaller groups inside them. There were voice and video calls, notifications, Google account linking, a rewards program where users could earn airtime, and yes, GoCredits were still there.

The app was still lightweight too.

Even more interesting, according to its website, 2go has recorded over 10 million all-time downloads, reached more than 50 countries, and continues to advertise an active community around the clock.

But here's what I couldn't find: how many people actually use 2go today?

That experience changed how I thought about the story. I went looking for a forgotten piece of internet history and found a platform that was still operating, still adapting, and still being used.

Perhaps It Was Never That Simple

I started this investigation wondering why 2go didn't evolve.

But it did.

It expanded to smartphones, introduced new features, and continued operating long after it lost the mainstream attention it once enjoyed.

Which brings me back to the question that started this whole investigation.

Did 2go fail to evolve, or did it successfully serve the purpose it was built for, at the time it was built, before the world moved on?

Maybe the answer lies somewhere in between.

Perhaps 2go was incredibly good at solving a particular problem for a particular generation, but maintaining that success required something different as technology and user behavior changed.

And perhaps losing popularity, failing to innovate, and failing as a business aren't necessarily the same thing.

I don't know if 2go could have become the next WhatsApp or Facebook. But I do know its story is more complicated than simply being replaced by another app.

And while Preciousred remains somewhere in my digital past, 2go is apparently still making new memories for someone else.
